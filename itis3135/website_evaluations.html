Website Evaluations

Ofrey.com

This is the website for an old watchmaker supply house. It says it's been around since 1930 and the website certainly looks like it has too.

  • Contrast: The website uses dark text on light backgrounds creating decent legibilty. The fonts used look to be a mix of generic san serif fonts like Arial, and Times New Roman, which also help for legibility. Overall, when it comes to legibility and contrast this website has no real issues. The site lacks a clear visual hierarchy. It heavily relies on massive blocks of bold text, generic blue links, and raw HTML formatting. Because almost everything is emphasized (large walls of bold text describing phone policies, ordering limits, and warnings), nothing actually stands out. Product numbers, dimensions, and prices blend together into a giant wall of text. There is virtually no contrasting background, size, or color hierarchy separating a product title from its description, SKU, or "Add to Cart" function. Important compatibility notices (e.g., whether a part is for an older, extinct movement) are formatted identically to standard stock text.
  • Repetition: While it has certain retro elements it lacks a cohesive design language. Fonts, spacing, and image sizes shift drastically across its thousands of subpages. Instead of creating a sense of unity or a professional brand identity, the erratic repetition makes the website feel like a patchwork of separate pages built across different decades..
  • Alignment: The site does not utilize a modern grid system. Text blocks, raw image links, and dividers are often center-aligned or awkwardly left-aligned without consistent margins. This creates a jagged, unorganized look. Without strong invisible vertical lines to anchor the content, the page layout feels chaotic, forcing the user to manually hunt through text to find what they need.
  • Proximity: Massive, multisentence paragraphs pack critical information like minimum order amounts ($20.00), operational hours, fax numbers, and advice for hobbyists closely together without sufficient whitespace or logical groupings. Related items are buried inside unrelated walls of text. For example, instead of a clean, dedicated "Contact Us" sidebar, phone rules and email restrictions are sandwiched into the main introductory text.

Nothing.tech

This is the website for a tech company called Nothing. They make phones, earbuds, headphones and other electronics.

  • Contrast: The site relies on a black and white color palette thats good for legibility. Elements are placed on opposing backgrounds to establish basic readability. Large scale product photography is contrasted against small, low weight typography that establishes a clear hierarchy where the imagery acts as the primary focal point, and text acts as a secondary element. The background fills are completely flat, which creates a tonal contrast against the highly detailed, multi-layered product renders.
  • Repetition: It has a dot matrix font that is repeated uniformly for major headers, price points, and navigational accents across all product pages. The site strictly repeats a system of one pixel technical grid lines to partition content. This specific border style is used to enclose text boxes, feature cards, and menu items globally. Minor visual details such as small red indicator dots, tiny crosshairs, and technical data labels are repeated across different pages to unify the interface styling
  • Alignment: The layout is built on a modular grid. Every text block, image edge, and boundary line aligns precisely with neighboring modules along vertical and horizontal axes. Text within the grid modules is generally left aligned to establish a consistent starting point for reading, matching the rigid edges of the containers. During animations, the focal points of the products are aligned to the exact center vertical axis of the viewport, maintaining a fixed structural path as the user scrolls.
  • Proximity: Individual technical specifications are placed inside a single, self-contained box. This closeness signals that the data points belong to the same feature. Massive blocks of empty space are used to separate major content sections. By spacing out the grid modules, the layout prevents different product features from bleeding into one another visually.
